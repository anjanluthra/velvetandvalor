Equestrian Terms: The Complete Glossary

From the arena to the stable yard, equestrian language has a precision and poetry all its own. This definitive glossary covers every term you need, whether you're a complete novice or a seasoned competitor.

Kate Luthra 24 June 2026 14 min read
Share

Key Takeaways

  • Equestrian vocabulary spans disciplines — dressage, showjumping, eventing, polo, and western riding each carry their own specialist terms, yet share a common foundation.
  • Understanding the correct terminology helps you communicate clearly with instructors, vets, farriers, and fellow riders, making you safer and more effective in the saddle.
  • Many equestrian terms derive from French, reflecting the profound influence of the French riding academies on classical horsemanship from the 16th century onwards.
  • The bond between horse and rider is at the heart of every term in this glossary — language that honours that relationship is language worth learning properly.

Walk into any stable yard and within minutes you will hear a language unlike any other — clipped, precise, and steeped in centuries of tradition. Equestrian terms are the shared vocabulary of an entire culture: they describe how a horse moves, how a rider communicates, how a yard is managed, and how competitions are judged. Whether you have just booked your first lesson or you are preparing for your next affiliated dressage test, a thorough grasp of equestrian terms will make you a more confident, more capable, and more respected member of the equestrian community. This glossary is designed to be the most comprehensive reference you will find — organised by theme, explained with genuine context, and written for riders of every level.

The Language of Movement: Gaits and Paces

A horse's natural gaits are the foundation of everything in equestrian sport. Understanding them precisely — not just by name but by beat, rhythm, and quality — is the mark of a genuinely educated rider.

The Four Natural Gaits

  • Walk — A four-beat gait in which each hoof strikes the ground separately. A correct walk has a clear, even rhythm with no moment of suspension.
  • Trot — A two-beat diagonal gait in which the horse springs from one diagonal pair of legs to the other, with a moment of suspension between each beat. Riders may sit to the trot or rise (post) in rhythm with it.
  • Canter — A three-beat gait with a moment of suspension. The sequence of footfalls begins with a single hind leg, followed by a diagonal pair, then the leading foreleg. Which foreleg leads matters enormously: on a circle to the left, the horse should be on the left lead.
  • Gallop — A four-beat extension of the canter, the horse's fastest gait, in which all four legs act independently.

Collection and Extension

These are among the most misunderstood equestrian terms for newcomers. Collection does not mean slowing down — it means the horse carries more weight on its hindquarters, shortens its frame, and elevates its steps. Extension is the opposite: the horse lengthens its frame and reaches further with each stride while maintaining rhythm. Both are asked for in dressage tests and are indicators of genuine training.

Western Gaits

In western riding disciplines, the terminology shifts. The trot becomes the jog (a slower, more collected version), and the canter becomes the lope. These are not merely different words for the same thing — they describe subtly different ways of going that suit the working ranch horse tradition.

Aids: How Rider and Horse Communicate

The aids are the signals a rider uses to communicate with a horse. Mastery of the aids is the lifelong pursuit of every serious equestrian.

Natural Aids

  • Leg aids — Applied behind the girth to ask for impulsion, sideways movement, or bend. The position of the leg (at the girth, behind it, or further back) changes the message.
  • Seat aids — Often the most powerful and least visible. A weighted seat bone, a following or stilling pelvis — these communicate directly through the horse's back.
  • Hand aids — Transmitted through the reins to the bit. Good hands are quiet, elastic, and never fixed; they receive and give rather than pull.
  • Voice — Permitted in some disciplines (lungeing, western classes) and forbidden in others (dressage competition). A calm voice is one of the most effective tools in early training.

Artificial Aids

  • Whip (schooling whip or jumping bat) — Used to reinforce the leg, never to punish.
  • Spurs — An extension of the leg aid, requiring considerable skill to use correctly.
  • Martingale — A piece of tack that limits how high a horse can raise its head; running and standing martingales serve slightly different purposes.

Dressage Terms: The Art of Classical Riding

Dressage — from the French dressage, meaning "training" — is the discipline in which horse and rider perform a prescribed series of movements judged on quality, harmony, and correctness. Many of its terms remain in French to this day, a legacy of the great riding academies.

Movements and Figures

  • Piaffe — A highly collected, cadenced trot in place (or with minimal forward movement). One of the most demanding movements in Grand Prix dressage.
  • Passage — A slow, elevated, very cadenced trot with a prolonged moment of suspension.
  • Half-pass — A lateral movement in which the horse travels simultaneously forward and sideways, remaining parallel to the long side of the arena, crossing its legs in front of each other.
  • Tempi changes — Flying changes of lead performed in sequence: every four strides, every three, every two, or every stride (one-time changes).
  • Levade — A classical High School movement in which the horse raises its forehand and holds itself at approximately 45 degrees on deeply bent haunches. A genuine levade, as performed at the Spanish Riding School in Vienna, requires extraordinary strength and years of preparation.
  • Pirouette — A circle of the smallest possible radius, performed in walk or canter, in which the horse's hindlegs describe a tiny circle while the forehand moves around them.
  • Volte — A circle of 6, 8, or 10 metres in diameter — a fundamental school figure.
  • Renvers and Travers — Counter-shoulder-in and haunches-in respectively; lateral movements that develop suppleness and collection.

Arena Markers

A standard competition arena measures 20 × 60 metres and is marked with letters: A, K, V, E, S, H, C, M, R, B, P, F, plus X at the centre. A smaller arena (20 × 40 m) uses a reduced set. These letters allow dressage tests to specify precisely where each movement begins and ends. The origin of the specific letter sequence is debated among historians, but their use is now standardised by the Fédération Équestre Internationale (FEI).

Jumping Terms: Showjumping and Cross-Country

Showjumping Vocabulary

  • Oxer — A spread fence with two sets of poles. A square oxer has both sets at equal height; an ascending oxer has the back rail higher than the front.
  • Combination — Two or more fences set one or two strides apart, numbered as elements A, B, and C. Faults at any element count against the combination as a whole.
  • Refusal — When a horse stops in front of a fence without jumping it. Under current FEI rules, one refusal results in elimination in international competition.
  • Knockdown — A rail dislodged by the horse or rider; typically incurs four faults.
  • Clear round — A round completed without jumping faults or time faults.
  • Puissance — A power-jumping competition involving a progressively raised wall, testing the ultimate height a horse can clear.

Cross-Country Terms

  • Bounce — Two fences set so close together that the horse lands and immediately takes off again with no stride between them.
  • Drop fence — A fence where the landing is significantly lower than the take-off.
  • Coffin — A combination typically consisting of a rail, a ditch, and another rail, requiring great boldness and adjustability.
  • Optimum time — The time set by the course designer that a horse must meet on cross-country; exceeding it incurs time penalties.

Stable Management and Horse Care Terms

A rider who only knows arena vocabulary is half-educated. The stable yard has its own rich terminology, and understanding it is a matter of both horsemanship and welfare. The British Horse Society provides excellent welfare guidance that underpins much of modern stable management practice.

Daily Care

  • Mucking out — Removing soiled bedding and droppings from a stable.
  • Skipping out — Removing only the droppings (without full mucking out) to keep the bed fresh between full cleans.
  • Grooming — The full process of brushing, picking out hooves, and caring for the horse's coat, mane, and tail. Beyond appearance, grooming stimulates circulation and allows the handler to check for injuries or skin conditions daily.
  • Tacking up / untacking — Putting on or removing the saddle, bridle, and associated equipment.
  • Rugging — Fitting a rug (blanket) appropriate to the weather conditions and the horse's clipping status.

Feed and Nutrition

  • Hard feed — Concentrated feed such as nuts, cubes, or mixes, as opposed to forage.
  • Forage — Roughage in the form of hay, haylage, or grass — the foundation of any horse's diet.
  • Ad lib forage — Providing forage in unlimited quantities, reflecting current best practice for digestive and psychological health.
  • Condition scoring — A standardised system for assessing a horse's body fat coverage, typically on a scale of 0–5 (in the UK) or 1–9 (in the US Henneke system).

Health and Veterinary Terms

  • Lameness — Any deviation from a normal, sound gait caused by pain or mechanical dysfunction.
  • Colic — Abdominal pain in the horse, ranging from mild discomfort to a life-threatening surgical emergency. It remains one of the most common causes of equine mortality.
  • Laminitis — Inflammation of the sensitive laminae within the hoof, often triggered by dietary issues or systemic illness.
  • Flexion test — A veterinary examination in which a limb is held in a flexed position for a set period before the horse is trotted away, used to identify sources of lameness.
  • PPID (Pituitary Pars Intermedia Dysfunction) — Commonly called Cushing's disease; a hormonal condition prevalent in older horses, affecting coat, weight, and hoof health.

Conformation and Breed Terms

Conformation refers to the physical structure and proportions of a horse's body. Good conformation generally correlates with soundness and athletic ability, though no horse is perfect.

  • Withers — The highest point of the horse's back, at the base of the neck, from which height is measured in hands (1 hand = 4 inches / 10.16 cm).
  • Cannon bone — The bone between the knee (or hock) and the fetlock; its length and density are important conformation considerations.
  • Pastern — The sloping bone between the fetlock and the hoof. A well-angled pastern (roughly 45–50 degrees) absorbs concussion efficiently.
  • Dished face — A concave facial profile, characteristic of the Arabian breed.
  • Roman nose — A convex facial profile, often seen in draft breeds.
  • Cold blood / Warm blood / Hot blood — These are temperament and type classifications rather than temperature references. Hot bloods (Thoroughbreds, Arabians) are fine-boned and reactive; cold bloods (draft breeds) are heavy and calm; warmbloods are purpose-bred crosses combining scope with trainability, and dominate modern sport horse competition.

Competition and Judging Terms

  • Affiliated competition — A competition run under the rules of a national or international governing body (such as British Dressage, British Showjumping, or the FEI), as opposed to an unaffiliated or grassroots show.
  • Qualifier — A competition whose results determine eligibility for a higher-level championship.
  • Hors concours (HC) — A competitor who takes part but is not eligible for prizes, often because they are competing above their registered level.
  • Impulsion — The energy that flows from the hindquarters through a supple back and into the contact. Critically, impulsion is not speed — a horse can have enormous impulsion at a collected pace.
  • Submission — In dressage judging, the horse's willingness and attentiveness to the rider's aids.
  • Suppleness — Lateral and longitudinal elasticity in the horse's body; one of the foundational scales of training.
  • The Training Scale — A hierarchical framework (Rhythm, Suppleness, Contact, Impulsion, Straightness, Collection) used to assess and develop a horse's education. It originated in German classical tradition and is now used worldwide.

Tack, Equipment, and Attire Terms

The craft of equestrian tack-making is one of the world's great leather traditions, and the vocabulary around it is precise. A full-grain leather bridle, for instance, is made from the outermost layer of the hide — the strongest, most breathable, and most beautiful layer — and ages into a lustre that synthetic materials simply cannot replicate.   • Saddle tree — The rigid internal framework of a saddle, traditionally made from beech wood or fibreglass, which distributes the rider's weight across the horse's back.
  • Panel — The padded underside of the saddle that sits against the horse's back; must be flocked or foam-filled to the correct profile for each individual horse.
  • Gullet — The channel running along the underside of the saddle that allows clearance over the horse's spine.
  • Stirrup iron — The metal loop in which the rider places their foot; safety stirrups are designed to release in a fall.
  • Bit — The metal piece that sits in the horse's mouth and transmits rein aids. Bits range from the mild loose-ring snaffle to the highly leverage-based Pelham or Kimblewick.
  • Numnah / Saddle pad — A shaped pad worn beneath the saddle to protect the horse's back and absorb sweat.
  • Girth — The strap that holds the saddle in place by fastening around the horse's barrel.
  • Breastplate — A piece of tack that prevents the saddle slipping backwards, particularly useful in cross-country.

Rider Attire

  • Breeches — Close-fitting riding trousers, typically with a reinforced inner leg.
  • Jodhpurs — Full-length riding trousers that extend into a cuff, worn with short jodhpur boots.
  • Showing jacket / Shadbelly — The tailored coat worn in the show ring; the shadbelly (a cutaway coat with tails) is worn at Grand Prix dressage level.
  • Stock tie — A formal white neckcloth secured with a pin, worn in dressage and showing classes.
  • Body protector — A padded safety vest worn for cross-country and jumping, rated to BETA standards in the UK.

Polo, Eventing, and Western: Specialist Vocabulary

Polo Terms

  • Chukka — A period of play in polo, lasting seven and a half minutes; a standard match comprises four to eight chukkas.
  • Handicap — Each polo player is assigned a handicap (–2 to 10 goals) reflecting their ability; team handicaps are combined to create a starting score difference.
  • Nearside / Offside — The left and right sides of the horse respectively; in polo, shots are named by which side of the horse they are played.

Eventing Terms

  • Three-day event — The combined test of dressage, cross-country, and showjumping; at the highest level (Badminton, Burghley, Luhmühlen), it remains one of sport's most demanding challenges.
  • Roads and tracks / Steeplechase — Phases that existed in the traditional long-format three-day event, largely removed from modern competition but still referenced in historical context.
  • Penalty zone — The marked area around a cross-country fence within which a refusal or run-out is penalised.

Western Riding Terms

  • Reining — A western discipline in which horse and rider perform patterns of spins, sliding stops, and lead changes; often described as the western equivalent of dressage.
  • Sliding stop — A reining movement in which the horse locks its hindlegs and slides to a halt, often covering several metres.
  • Spin — A rapid 360-degree turn on the haunches, performed in reining patterns.
  • Neck reining — Steering by laying the rein against the horse's neck rather than direct rein pressure, freeing the rider's hand for ranch work.
  • Hackamore — A bitless bridle that works on nose and chin pressure; widely used in western traditions and increasingly seen in other disciplines.

The Horse–Human Bond: Personal and Cultural Terms

Language also captures the emotional and cultural dimensions of equestrian life — the dimension that makes this world so enduring and so personal.

  • Partnership — Perhaps the most important word in equestrianism. A true partnership between horse and rider is built on trust, clear communication, and mutual respect; it is not the dominance of one over the other.
  • Horseman / Horsewoman / Horseperson — Someone whose understanding of the horse goes beyond riding technique to encompass behaviour, welfare, and intuition.
  • Horse whisperer — A popular term for a trainer who uses natural horsemanship methods; the more precise term is natural horsemanship practitioner.
  • Yard culture — The informal customs, hierarchies, and social norms of a stable yard; a rich world in itself.
  • Colours — In racing, the registered silks worn by a jockey to identify the horse's owner.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most important equestrian terms a beginner should learn first?

Start with the aids (leg, seat, hand, and voice), the basic gaits (walk, trot, canter, and gallop), and stable management terms such as mucking out, grooming, and tacking up. These form the practical vocabulary you'll use every single day.

Why do so many equestrian terms come from French?

The French riding academies — most notably the École de Cavalerie at Saumur, founded in 1763 — codified classical horsemanship and published influential texts that spread their terminology across Europe. Words such as levade, piaffe, and passage entered the international lexicon through that tradition and have remained ever since.

Are equestrian terms the same across all disciplines?

Core terms (gaits, aids, arena markers) are shared, but each discipline adds its own specialist vocabulary. A western rider speaks of a lope rather than a canter, a polo player refers to chukkas and mallets, and a dressage judge scores movements such as half-pass and tempi changes that a showjumper may never practise.

What is the difference between a horse and a pony in equestrian terminology?

The distinction is primarily one of height. In most equestrian contexts, an animal standing 14.2 hands (hh) or under is classified as a pony; above that height it is a horse. One hand equals four inches (approximately 10.16 cm). Breed characteristics and bone density also factor into formal classification for competition purposes.

How do arena letters help riders in equestrian training?

Arena letters mark fixed points around the perimeter and centre line of a standard manège, allowing instructors to give precise directions ("turn left at E," "begin your canter at K") and enabling dressage tests to specify exactly where each movement should start and finish. The standard 20 × 60 m arena uses the letters A, K, V, E, S, H, C, M, R, B, P, and F, plus X at the centre.
