Equestrian Terms: The Complete Glossary

From "on the bit" to "oxer," equestrian language has a vocabulary all its own. This definitive glossary covers every term you need — whether you're a curious newcomer or a seasoned rider brushing up on the finer points.

Kate Luthra 24 June 2026 16 min read
Share

Key Takeaways

  • Equestrian terms span multiple disciplines — dressage, show jumping, eventing, and Western riding each have their own distinct vocabulary.
  • Understanding tack terminology is as important as knowing the riding terms themselves; the two are inseparable in practice.
  • Many equestrian terms derive from French, reflecting the historical dominance of the French riding academy tradition in classical horsemanship.
  • Knowing the correct terminology signals respect for the sport's heritage and helps you communicate precisely with instructors, vets, and fellow riders.

Walk into any yard, arena, or saddlery and you will encounter a language that has been refined over centuries — precise, layered, and deeply satisfying once you begin to understand it. Equestrian terms are the shared vocabulary of everyone who works with horses, from the groom mucking out at dawn to the Grand Prix dressage rider preparing a piaffe. Whether you are entirely new to the equestrian world or returning to riding after years away, having a reliable reference to hand makes every lesson, every yard conversation, and every competition programme considerably more rewarding. This is that reference: a thorough, plainly written guide to equestrian terms across disciplines, covering gaits, tack, arena work, competition, conformation, and stable management — with nothing important left out.

The Language of Movement: Gaits and Paces

The gaits are the foundation of all riding, and getting their definitions right matters from day one.

Walk is a four-beat gait in which each hoof strikes the ground separately. It is the slowest gait but requires as much rider attention as any other; a good walk is free, regular, and swinging.

Trot is a two-beat diagonal gait: the horse springs from one diagonal pair of legs to the other with a moment of suspension between. Riders either sit the trot (absorbing the movement through the seat) or rise (posting up and down in rhythm with the outside foreleg).

Canter is a three-beat gait with a moment of suspension. The sequence of footfalls depends on the lead — the foreleg that appears to reach furthest forward. On a right lead, the sequence is left hind, right hind and left fore together, then right fore (the leading leg), followed by suspension.

Gallop is a four-beat extension of the canter, the fastest natural gait of a horse. In competition, a hand gallop is a controlled, collected version used in some hunter classes.

Collected, medium, and extended are qualitative variations within a gait. Collection means the horse carries more weight on its hindquarters, shortening and elevating its steps; extension means the horse lengthens its stride to cover maximum ground while maintaining rhythm.

Specialist Gaits

Some breeds perform gaits beyond the standard four:

  • Pace — a lateral two-beat gait seen in Standardbreds used in harness racing.
  • Tölt — the smooth, four-beat running walk of the Icelandic Horse, prized for its comfort over long distances.
  • Paso llano / sobreandando — lateral gaits performed by Paso Fino and Peruvian Paso breeds.
  • Running walk — the signature gait of the Tennessee Walking Horse, ground-covering and supremely comfortable for trail riding.

Essential Tack Terminology

Tack is the collective noun for all the equipment placed on a horse for riding or driving. Understanding tack vocabulary is inseparable from understanding riding itself — your instructor will refer to these items constantly, and fitting tack correctly is a welfare matter, not merely a practical one. The British Horse Society offers detailed guidance on correct tack fitting as part of its welfare resources.

The Saddle

  • Tree — the internal frame of the saddle, traditionally made from beech wood or fibreglass, which determines its shape and fit.
  • Panel — the padded underside of the saddle that sits against the horse's back.
  • Gullet — the channel running along the underside of the saddle; it must clear the horse's spine completely.
  • Girth — the strap that passes under the horse's belly to secure the saddle. Western riders call this a cinch.
  • Stirrup iron / stirrup leather — the metal loop in which the rider places their foot, suspended from the saddle by a leather strap.
  • Pommel — the front arch of the saddle. Cantle — the rear arch.
  • Seat — the central area where the rider sits. A deep seat means the lowest point is well defined, encouraging the rider to sit centrally.

The Bridle

  • Headpiece (crownpiece) — the strap that sits behind the horse's ears and bears the weight of the bridle.
  • Browband — the strap across the forehead, which prevents the headpiece sliding back.
  • Cheekpieces — the straps connecting the headpiece to the bit.
  • Noseband — the strap around the nose. Types include the cavesson (plain), flash (with a lower strap), drop, Grackle (figure-of-eight), and Micklem.
  • Bit — the metal (or synthetic) mouthpiece through which rein aids are transmitted. Common types: snaffle (direct action), pelham (combines snaffle and curb action in one bit), double bridle (uses both a snaffle — called the bridoon — and a curb bit — the Weymouth).
  • Reins — the straps connecting the bit to the rider's hands.

Additional Equipment

  • Martingale — a training aid that limits how high the horse can raise its head. A running martingale works through the reins; a standing martingale attaches to the noseband.
  • Breastplate — a strap across the chest preventing the saddle from slipping back.
  • Numah / saddle pad — the cloth placed under the saddle to absorb sweat and protect the horse's back.
  • Boots and bandages — protective coverings for the horse's legs. Types include brushing boots (protect against the horse's own hooves), tendon boots, overreach boots (bell-shaped, protecting the heel), and exercise bandages.

Arena and Schooling Terms

The arena (also called the school or manège) has its own geography and vocabulary, standardised internationally so that a rider trained in one country can follow instructions anywhere in the world.

Arena Letters and Markers

A standard dressage arena is 20 × 40 metres (small arena) or 20 × 60 metres (large arena). Lettered markers placed around the perimeter guide movements. The letters in a small arena are A, K, E, H, C, M, B, F; the large arena adds V, P, S, R, I, L, X, G, D. X marks the centre of the arena; G and D are on the centre line. The origin of many of these letters is debated — one credible account traces them to German cavalry commands, though no single authoritative source has settled the question definitively.

Common Schooling Movements

  • Circle — a round figure ridden at a specified diameter (6m, 10m, 15m, or 20m). Precision of shape is a training test in itself.
  • Serpentine — a series of equal loops across the school, requiring changes of bend.
  • Leg yield — a lateral movement in which the horse moves forward and sideways, crossing its legs, while remaining parallel to the long side. It is a foundational exercise for suppleness.
  • Shoulder-in — the horse's forehand is brought slightly off the track, creating a three-track movement with bend away from the direction of travel. Attributed to François Robichon de la Guérinière (1688–1751), it remains the cornerstone of classical lateral work.
  • Travers (haunches-in) — the opposite of shoulder-in; the hindquarters are brought in off the track.
  • Renvers (haunches-out) — similar to travers but ridden with the horse's head to the wall.
  • Half-pass — the horse moves forward and sideways simultaneously, bent in the direction of travel. A more advanced version of leg yield.
  • Transition — any change between or within gaits. Upward transitions increase gait; downward transitions decrease. Direct transitions skip intermediate gaits (e.g., walk to canter).
  • On the bit — a state of acceptance and engagement in which the horse carries itself in a round outline, responds softly to the contact, and works through its back. Not to be confused with behind the bit (horse evades by tucking its nose behind the vertical) or above the bit (horse raises its head to avoid contact).
  • Impulsion — the energy flowing from the hindquarters forward. Often confused with speed; a horse can have tremendous impulsion at a slow pace.
  • Collection — the redistribution of the horse's weight onto its hindquarters, resulting in elevated, shorter steps and a lighter forehand.

Dressage Terms

Dressage — from the French dresser, meaning to train — is the discipline most closely associated with classical equestrian terms. Governed internationally by the Fédération Equestre Internationale (FEI), dressage tests are scored by judges on a scale of 0–10 for each movement.

The Grand Prix Movements

  • Piaffe — a highly collected trot in place (or with minimal forward movement). The horse must show clear diagonal pairs and a moment of suspension.
  • Passage — a very collected, elevated trot with a prolonged moment of suspension. The transition between piaffe and passage (P-P transition) is one of the most demanding sequences in sport.
  • Pirouette — a turn on the haunches in which the horse completes a small circle with its hind legs while the forehand moves around them. Performed in walk and canter.
  • Flying change — a change of canter lead during the moment of suspension. Tempi changes are sequences of flying changes every stride (ones), every two strides (twos), every three strides (threes), or every four strides (fours).
  • Half-pass in canter — a canter half-pass is judged on the degree of bend, the crossing of the legs, and the maintenance of rhythm.

Judges' Terminology

  • Submission — the horse's willingness and acceptance; not passive obedience, but active cooperation.
  • Regularity — the purity and evenness of the gait's rhythm and footfalls.
  • Throughness (Durchlässigkeit) — the unobstructed flow of energy and aids from back to front and front to back through a supple, swinging back.

Show Jumping and Eventing Terms

Show Jumping

  • Oxer — a spread fence with two sets of poles. A square oxer has front and back rails at equal height; a Swedish oxer has the rails set diagonally, creating a V-shape.
  • Vertical (upright) — a fence with a single set of poles directly above one another, testing precision of height.
  • Liverpool — a fence incorporating a water tray beneath the poles.
  • Combination — two or more fences set at one or two strides apart, numbered as A, B, C. A double is two elements; a treble is three.
  • Fault — a penalty. Knocking a pole down or refusing at a fence typically incurs 4 faults; a fall incurs elimination in most competitions.
  • Clear round — completion of the course with no faults; in a jump-off, the fastest clear round wins.
  • Course walk — the period before competition in which riders walk the course on foot to plan their lines and count strides between fences.
  • Related distance — the measured number of strides between two fences that are not a combination but are close enough to influence each other.

Eventing (Three-Day Eventing)

Eventing combines dressage, cross-country, and show jumping over one or more days. The discipline is sometimes called combined training.

  • Cross-country — the second phase, ridden across natural terrain with fixed, solid obstacles. Penalties are incurred for refusals and exceeding the optimum time.
  • Optimum time — the target time calculated from the set distance and speed. Going over time incurs time penalties; going under (in most modern formats) does not.
  • Technical delegate (TD) — the official responsible for ensuring the course and competition comply with the rules.
  • Coffin — a cross-country combination typically comprising a fence, a ditch, and another fence in close succession.
  • Trakehner — a suspended rail over a ditch, named after the German Trakehner breed's homeland. One of the more technically demanding cross-country fence types.

Conformation and Veterinary Terms

Conformation refers to the physical structure and proportions of the horse. Good conformation generally correlates with soundness and athletic potential, though exceptions are plentiful.

  • Withers — the highest point of the back, located at the base of the neck between the shoulder blades. Horse height is measured in hands (1 hand = 4 inches / 10.16 cm) from the ground to the withers.
  • Cannon bone — the large bone between the knee (front) or hock (hind) and the fetlock.
  • Fetlock — the joint between the cannon bone and the pastern, roughly equivalent to the human ankle.
  • Pastern — the short bone between the fetlock and the hoof. A long, sloping pastern absorbs concussion well; a short, upright pastern transmits more concussion to the leg.
  • Hock — the large joint on the hind leg, equivalent to the human heel. Common issues include bone spavin (arthritis of the lower hock joints) and bog spavin (excess joint fluid).
  • Stifle — the joint on the hind leg equivalent to the human knee.
  • Soundness — the absence of lameness or disease. A horse is described as sound when it moves without restriction or pain.
  • Lameness — an alteration in gait caused by pain or mechanical dysfunction. Graded on a scale of 0–5 by the American Association of Equine Practitioners (AAEP), from 0 (no lameness) to 5 (non-weight-bearing).
  • Colic — abdominal pain, one of the most common and potentially serious equine health emergencies. Types include spasmodic, impaction, displacement, and torsion (twisted gut).
  • Wind — refers to a horse's respiratory capacity. A broken-winded horse suffers from heaves (equine asthma); roaring (laryngeal hemiplegia) is a condition causing an abnormal respiratory noise on exertion.

Western Riding Terms

Western riding developed from the working traditions of the American ranch, and its vocabulary reflects practical cattle-handling origins.

  • Lope — the Western equivalent of the canter; generally ridden at a slower, more relaxed tempo.
  • Jog — the Western equivalent of the trot, slow and smooth.
  • Reining — a Western discipline of precise patterns including spins, sliding stops, and rollbacks, often described as the Western equivalent of dressage.
  • Sliding stop — the signature reining movement in which the horse locks its hindquarters and slides to a halt across the arena, sometimes covering ten metres or more.
  • Spin (pirouette) — a rapid rotation on the hindquarters, scored on speed and correctness.
  • Neck reining — a method of steering in which the rein is laid against the horse's neck rather than pulled directly; the horse moves away from the rein pressure.
  • Hackamore — a bitless bridle that works through pressure on the nose and chin. Traditional Western hackamores use a bosal (braided rawhide nosepiece); the mechanical hackamore uses shanks for leverage.
  • Cutting — the discipline in which a horse works to separate a single cow from a herd and prevent it from returning, guided only minimally by the rider once the cow is selected.
  • Barrel racing — a timed event in which horse and rider navigate a cloverleaf pattern around three barrels.

Stable Management and Yard Terms

The vocabulary of the yard is where equestrian life is lived day-to-day — and where a practitioner's knowledge shows most clearly.

  • Mucking out — the daily removal of soiled bedding and droppings from a stable. Skipping out refers to removing droppings between full mucks.
  • Bedding — the material covering the stable floor. Options include straw, shavings, paper, hemp, and rubber matting.
  • Hay and haylage — conserved forage. Hay is dried grass; haylage is partially wilted and wrapped grass with higher moisture content and a lower dust content, often preferred for horses with respiratory sensitivities.
  • Hard feed (concentrate) — grain-based or compound feeds given to supplement forage. Types include nuts/cubes, mix, chaff, and straights (single ingredients such as oats or sugar beet).
  • Turnout — time spent by the horse at liberty in a field or paddock. Adequate turnout is considered fundamental to equine welfare.
  • Rugging — the practice of placing a rug (blanket) on a horse. Types include turnout rugs (waterproof, for outdoor use), stable rugs (non-waterproof, for indoor use), fly rugs, and exercise sheets.
  • Grooming — the daily care of the horse's coat, mane, tail, and hooves. Tools include the dandy brush, body brush, curry comb, mane comb, hoof pick, and stable rubber.
  • Farriery — the skilled trade of shoeing and trimming horses' hooves. A farrier fits metal shoes; a barefoot trimmer maintains the unshod hoof. In the UK, farriery is a regulated profession under the Farriers (Registration) Act 1975.
  • Box rest — confinement of a horse to its stable on veterinary advice, typically during recovery from injury.
  • Clipping — the removal of the winter coat using electric clippers to allow a horse in work to cool down more efficiently and dry faster. Common clip types: full clip, hunter clip, blanket clip, trace clip, and chaser clip.

Breed and Registration Terms

  • Warmblood — a category of sport horse bred from crosses between hotbloods (Thoroughbred, Arabian) and coldbloods (draft breeds). Major European studbooks include the KWPN (Dutch), Hanoverian, Oldenburg, and Westphalian.
  • Thoroughbred — a breed developed in England for racing, tracing to three foundation sires: the Darley Arabian, Byerley Turk, and Godolphin Arabian.
  • Part-bred — a horse with some but not full registration in a named breed studbook.
  • Mare — an adult female horse (four years and older in most breed registries). Stallion — an uncastrated adult male. Gelding — a castrated male. Colt — a young male under four. Filly — a young female under four. Foal — a horse of either sex under one year. Weanling — a foal that has been weaned. Yearling — a horse in its first full year after the year of birth.
  • Dam — a horse's mother. Sire — a horse's father. Out of / by — pedigree shorthand: a horse is described as being by its sire and out of its dam.
  • Brand — a permanent mark used to identify horses in some breed registries and countries. Freeze-branding (using extreme cold rather than heat) is increasingly common as it is considered less painful.

A Note on Using Equestrian Terms Well

Vocabulary is a tool, not a test. The riders and horsewomen who use equestrian terms most effectively are those who understand the why behind each word — why a horse must be through before it can be truly collected, why a travers differs meaningfully from a renvers, why the distinction between a snaffle and a pelham matters for a specific horse's way of going. The terminology exists to make precise communication possible, not to exclude newcomers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most important equestrian terms for a complete beginner?

Begin with the gaits (walk, trot, canter, gallop), the basic tack vocabulary (saddle, bridle, girth, stirrup), and directional aids (leg, rein, seat). Once those are solid, move on to arena terminology such as "track," "centre line," and "working trot."

Why do so many equestrian terms come from French?

The French riding tradition, codified at the Académie d'équitation in Versailles during the 17th century, became the gold standard for classical horsemanship across Europe. Terms like "piaffe," "passage," and "pirouette" entered the international lexicon through that influence and were formalised by the Fédération Equestre Internationale (FEI) as the sport globalised.

What is the difference between a horse's "gait" and its "pace"?

"Gait" is the umbrella term for any pattern of movement — walk, trot, canter, gallop. "Pace" is a specific lateral two-beat gait in which both legs on the same side move together, seen in harness racing breeds such as the Standardbred. In casual speech, "pace" is sometimes used loosely to mean speed, but in equestrian contexts precision matters.

What does "on the bit" mean?

A horse is "on the bit" when it accepts the contact of the rider's hands through the reins, carries itself in a round, balanced outline, and responds softly to rein aids. It is not about forcing the head down; it describes a state of genuine engagement and self-carriage.

Are Western and English equestrian terms interchangeable?

Only partially. Both disciplines share anatomical and stable-management vocabulary, but tack, movement, and competition terms differ considerably. A "lope" in Western riding is the equivalent of a "canter" in English; a Western "jog" corresponds to a "trot." Equipment terms diverge too — a Western "cinch" is the English "girth."
